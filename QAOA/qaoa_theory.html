QAOA theory · from magnets to a working circuit

Why every piece of the circuit is there

You already understand MaxCut, and the spec already lists the circuit's parts. This page is the missing middle: the actual chain of reasoning that turns "magnets touching each other" into that exact circuit — so every gate has a reason, not just a name.

Act 1 · Physics

Give the magnet story a name and a formula

Recall the magnets: each one points up or down, and touching magnets are calmest when they disagree. Let's write that down as a number instead of a feeling.

H(s)  =  Σtouching pairs   si · sj    where each s is +1 (up) or −1 (down) For every touching pair, multiply their two directions. Same direction gives +1 (uncomfortable). Opposite directions give −1 (comfortable). Add it all up — that total is called the energy, and physics always tries to make it as low as possible.

This setup — spins, touching pairs, this add-em-up rule — has a name: the Ising model. It's one of the most-studied objects in physics, mostly because this simple magnet toy turns out to describe a surprising range of real behavior, optimization problems included.

Act 2 · Translation

The one-line algebra that turns magnets into MaxCut

Split every touching pair into two buckets: same (contributes +1) or cut, i.e. disagreeing (contributes −1). Then:

H(s) = (same pairs) − (cut pairs) = (total pairs − cut) − cut = (total pairs) − 2·(cut) The total number of touching pairs never changes. So the only way to make H smaller is to make the cut count bigger. Minimizing energy and maximizing cuts are the same goal, wearing different clothes.

Solve that last line for the cut count and you get exactly the cost function already sitting in your spec file:

C(z) = (total pairs − H(s)) / 2 = Σ(i,j)∈E (1 − zizj) / 2 It didn't come from nowhere — it's just "solve the magnet equation for the number of cuts."

Check it on a tiny graph — three nodes, all pairwise touching (a triangle):

H = −1 − 1 + 1 = −1. Total pairs = 3. Formula gives C(z) = (3 − (−1)) / 2 = 2. Counting cut pairs directly in the table also gives 2. They agree — the formula isn't a leap of faith, it's arithmetic.

Act 3 · The quantum swap

Turning z into a quantum operator

Classically, zi is just a number sitting in a spreadsheet cell: +1 or −1. To make it quantum, promote it to an operator Zi — a tiny instruction that acts on one qubit and returns +1 if that qubit is in state |0⟩, or −1 if it's in state |1⟩.

That's the entire quantum "trick," stated plainly: |0⟩ and |1⟩ literally are the two spin directions. A row of n qubits, each sitting in |0⟩ or |1⟩, is exactly one specific magnet arrangement — one specific coloring. Nothing exotic yet.

Here's where it gets genuinely different from a classical computer. A classical machine can only hold one coloring in memory at a time — that's why brute force means checking colorings one after another, 2ⁿ of them. A quantum computer can hold a weighted mix — a superposition — of all 2ⁿ colorings at once, as a single state. That's the actual advantage on the table: not "a faster processor," but "every coloring's slot exists simultaneously instead of one at a time."

Add up ZiZj terms the same way as the magnet formula, and you get an operator C, built purely from Z's. Applied to a superposition, C doesn't move any probability around — but internally, it "remembers" each coloring's own cut score. There's just one problem: C on its own isn't a valid quantum operation (physical operations must preserve total probability; C, as written, doesn't). The fix is a classic one:

U(C,γ) = e−iγC Any number shaped like ei×(real number) always has size exactly 1 — a fact about circles, not physics. So exponentiating turns each coloring's cost into a phase (a direction, not a size): automatically valid, and it tags every coloring in the superposition with its own cost, all at once, in one gate.
Act 4 · Why alternate

The slow-dial idea

There are now two Hamiltonians in play: the cost one you just built (hard — you don't know its lowest point in advance, that's the whole problem), and a mixer one, built from X operators instead of Z's, chosen specifically because it's easy — its own natural resting configuration is exactly the equal superposition you start the circuit in.

Imagine a dial. At position 0, you're sitting in the easy mixer problem, whose best answer you already know for free (equal superposition). At position 1, you're sitting in the hard cost problem. Physics has a real theorem here: if you turn the dial from 0 to 1 slowly enough, the system stays in its best possible configuration the entire way — so by the time you reach position 1, you're sitting in the hard problem's best configuration too, without ever having searched for it directly. (This is called the adiabatic theorem, and the recipe built on it is adiabatic optimization — the name is worth knowing even though we won't derive it further here.)

A truly slow, continuous dial-turn needs an impractically long circuit. QAOA's move is to fake it with a few big, deliberate jumps instead: a chunk of "hard" (U(C,γ)), then a chunk of "easy" (U(B,β)), repeated p times. The size of each jump — γ and β — isn't fixed by a slow schedule; it's left free, and the classical optimizer's entire job is to find the p jump-sizes that land closest to the hard problem's best configuration in just those few steps.

That's the whole theory, start to finish: magnets give you an energy formula, algebra turns that energy into a cut-count, promoting z to Z turns the cut-count into an operator, exponentiating makes that operator a legal quantum gate, and the adiabatic idea explains why you alternate it with a second, easy operator instead of applying it once and measuring.
