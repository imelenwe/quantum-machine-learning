QAOA / MaxCut · classical vs quantum

Two ways to search the same 64 answers

Same graph, same cost formula, same 64 possible colorings. This page walks through the classical way of solving it, then the quantum way, on their own terms — then lines them up to see exactly where they agree, where they don't, and why they're not really two different algorithms at all. Every number below came out of QAOA_MaxCut.ipynb, nothing here is illustrative.

6 nodes means 2 choices per node, so 2⁶ = 64 possible ways to split them into two teams. Each split "cuts" some number of the 8 rivalry edges — the ones where the two ends land on different teams. The question is the same for both approaches below: which of the 64 splits cuts the most edges?

Part 1 Classical — try everyone, one at a time

The brute-force answer is exactly what it sounds like: loop over all 64 splits, count how many edges each one cuts, keep the best. There's no cleverness to it — every single split gets visited and scored.

for state in range(64): # every possible split, one by one
  z = split_into_teams(state) # e.g. [+1,-1,+1,+1,-1,-1]
  cost = sum((1 - z[i]*z[j]) / 2 for i, j in edges)
  costs.append(cost)

(1 - z_i·z_j)/2 is just a switch: it's 1 when nodes i and j land on different teams (that edge got cut — good), and 0 when they're on the same team (not cut). Summed over all 8 edges, that's the cut count for one split.

The winner: split #25 (and its twin, #38) cut 7 of the 8 rivalries — team {1, 2, 5} vs. team {0, 3, 4}. Only rivalry 1–2 stays uncut — those two ended up on the same team, no way around it.
What this buys you: exact and guaranteed — there is zero doubt #25/#38 are the true best, because every single split got checked. What it costs: the loop runs 2ⁿ times. 64 iterations for 6 nodes is nothing; 60 nodes would be 2⁶⁰ ≈ 10¹⁸ iterations — no computer finishes that loop.
Part 2 Quantum — tilt all 64 at once

The quantum circuit never loops over splits. It builds one 6-qubit state that represents all 64 splits simultaneously, then uses interference to lean that state's weight toward the good splits, without ever visiting them one at a time.

Step 1 — superposition. A layer of Hadamard gates puts the state into an equal mix of all 64 basis states. Every split starts out equally likely: probability 1/64 each.

Step 2 — the cost, written as phase. The exact same formula from Part 1 — (1 - z_i·z_j)/2 per edge — gets applied to the state through the CX–Rz(−γ)–CX gadget proven in the phase-gate proof. But it lands as an angle twisted into each split's amplitude, not a change in height. The bar chart above wouldn't move an inch here — a good split and a bad split still look equally likely, the difference is hidden in a phase you can't see by looking at probabilities alone.

Step 3 — the mixer turns phase into probability. A layer of X-rotations lets those hidden phase differences interfere — splits whose phases lined up favorably gain real probability weight, others lose it. This is the one step that actually reshapes the bar chart.

What this buys you: a state that leans, more and more with each round, toward the true best split — without ever checking splits one at a time. What it costs: it's a heuristic — the expectation value climbs toward 7 but the run above never quite reaches full certainty even at p=5. The price for more rounds is circuit depth, not loop iterations: 20 → 84 gates deep across p=1…5 here, and every extra gate is another chance for real hardware to introduce error.

1

The expectation value is not the answer. 6.72 at p=5 isn't a split that cuts 6.72 edges — that number doesn't correspond to any real split. It's the average cost you'd get if you sampled the circuit's current state many times. The actual answer is still a specific bitstring, read out separately.

2

Reading out that bitstring costs something too. This page peeked at the exact statevector to find max_prob_index — a simulator-only shortcut. Real hardware only gives you samples, one shot at a time, and needs enough of them to trust which bitstring is really the most frequent.

3

#25 and #38 are the same answer wearing two labels. They're exact bit-flips of each other — team A and team B swapped. The cost formula can't tell them apart, so the circuit gives them identical probability at every single p above. Not a bug — the graph genuinely has no way to prefer one team-labeling over the other.

4

This demo doesn't show quantum "winning." At 6 nodes, the classical loop finishes instantly and is certain; the quantum circuit is slower to build and only approximate. The actual argument for quantum here is about scaling — circuit depth growing far more gently than 2ⁿ — which only starts to matter at problem sizes neither of us can brute-force by hand.

They're the same landscape. Overlay the classical cost bars with the quantum probability at p=5, same x-axis, and the shape lines up: probability mass piles up exactly where the cost bars are tallest.

That's not a coincidence to explain away — it's the same cut_count formula underneath both charts. Classical evaluates it in a Python loop, one split at a time. Quantum exponentiates it into a phase, once, and lets interference evaluate all 64 at once. Different computation, identical cost function — which is exactly why the two charts above land on the same peaks.
