QAOA proof · problem Hamiltonian & mixer, derived not asserted

From the exponential to the exact gates

Part 1 starts from the operator you already built, exponentiates it honestly, and watches the CX–Rz–CX gadget fall out as the only reasonable answer for the problem Hamiltonian operator — six small steps, nothing skipped. Part 2 picks up exactly where that leaves off: the phase is deposited, but invisible — measuring right now would show nothing has changed. It's the mixer operator that turns that hidden phase into an actual shift in probability, and that's where we watch constructive and destructive interference happen, both on paper and in a fully worked numeric example.

Where we're starting from C(Z) = Σedges (I − ZiZj) / 2     U(C,γ) = e−iγC(Z)
Part 1 of 2 · the problem Hamiltonian operator
Step 1 of 6

A sum in the exponent becomes a product of gates

e−iγ(A+B) = e−iγA · e−iγB    only when A and B commute (AB = BA) In general, exponentiating a sum of matrices is NOT the same as multiplying the separate exponentials — matrices don't usually commute, so order matters and messy cross-terms appear. There's exactly one clean exception: if A and B commute, the split works perfectly, no correction needed.

Every term in C(Z) is built purely from Z operators, on any qubits. Z operators always commute with each other — even ZiZj and ZiZk, which share qubit i. So this split isn't an approximation for us — it's exact:

e−iγC(Z) = e−iγ Σedges (I−ZiZj)/2 = ∏edges e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2 This is the single most important move in the whole proof: it means we never need one giant gate for the whole graph. We can build the circuit one edge at a time, in any order, and just multiply the small pieces together.
Step 2 of 6

Zoom into one edge

Take one factor from that product and split its exponent into two pieces:

e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2 = e−iγ/2 · e+iγZiZj/2 The first factor is just a plain number — identical for every possible state, since it doesn't depend on i or j at all. A phase that's the same everywhere is called a global phase, and it's invisible to any measurement — physically meaningless. Drop it.
This is the exact same move as dropping the constant when we turned the Ising energy into the MaxCut cost formula, all the way back at the start. It keeps showing up because it's a genuinely reusable trick, not a one-off.

What survives is the real work for this one edge:

e+iγZiZj/2
Step 3 of 6

What that surviving piece actually does

ZiZj is diagonal — you already know its eigenvalue on any state: +1 if i and j agree, −1 if they disagree. Exponentiating a diagonal matrix is easy: exponentiate each diagonal entry on its own, nothing fancier.

agree (00, 11)+1e+iγ/2

This is the actual target now, stated in plain terms: nudge every "agree" state by +γ/2, and every "disagree" state by −γ/2. The question becomes concrete: is there a real 2-qubit circuit that does exactly this?
Step 4 of 6

Testing the candidate circuit

Here's the candidate: a CNOT, a single-qubit Rz(θ) on the target only, then a second CNOT to undo the first.

Rz(θ) = e−iθ/20 0e+iθ/2
Top-left acts on |0⟩, bottom-right acts on |1⟩ — that's what "leave |0⟩ at e−iθ/2, tag |1⟩ with e+iθ/2" actually means, read straight off the diagonal. The off-diagonal zeros are just as important: they mean Rz never mixes |0⟩ and |1⟩ into each other, it only ever multiplies each by its own phase.

−θ/2 = +γ/2   ⇒   θ = −γ Both rows of the table agree on the same answer — one unknown, two consistent equations. The circuit matches the target exactly when the Rz angle is negative gamma, not gamma itself.

Worth knowing: the original lab notebook uses Rz(+γ) instead of Rz(−γ). That's not a mistake — it builds a sign-flipped version of the same idea, and since the classical optimizer searches over every value of γ anyway, it finds the equivalent answer either way. We're using −γ because we derived it ourselves and it's the version that matches our formula exactly, not because +γ would have failed.
Step 6 of 6

Reassembling the full operator

Walk back up the chain: one edge's gadget, with the angle now pinned down, is:

e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2  ≡  CX(i,j) · Rz(−γ)j · CX(i,j)    (up to the dropped global phase from Step 2)

And Step 1 said the whole operator is just the product of these, one per edge, in any order:

U(C,γ) = e−iγC(Z) = ∏(i,j) ∈ edges   CX(i,j) · Rz(−γ)j · CX(i,j)

What this means for the code, so far

The whole proof collapses into one sentence: for every edge in the graph, apply CX, then Rz(−γ) on the second qubit of that edge, then CX again — the exact same gadget, once per edge, back to back. That's the entire content of the problem_hamiltonian_operator function.

But notice what that gadget never touched: probability. Every step above only ever multiplied a basis state by a phase — a complex number of size 1. If you measured the circuit right after Part 1, every one of the 2n outcomes would still be exactly as likely as it was after the Hadamards. The cost function is in there, but it's invisible. Part 2 is about the gate that makes it visible.

Part 2 of 2 · the mixer operator
Where Part 2 starts from every state: same size, different phase     U(B,β) = Rx(β) applied to every qubit
Mixer step 1 of 2

The state Rx actually has to work with

Smallest case where the mixing is visible: 2 qubits, 1 edge — same gates as the real 6-qubit circuit, just small enough to expand by hand. From Step 5, one edge gives agree states (|00⟩, |11⟩) phase e+iγ/2 and disagree states (|01⟩, |10⟩) phase e−iγ/2. Factor out e+iγ/2 as a global phase (same drop-the-global-phase move as Step 2) and what's left is:

|ψ⟩ = ½[ |00⟩ + e−iγ|01⟩ + e−iγ|10⟩ + |11⟩ ] Agree states carry no extra twist (phase 1); disagree states carry an extra twist of e−iγ. That's the entire output of Part 1 for this example — still equal size on all four terms, only the phase differs. Measure right now and all four outcomes are still equally likely.

Rx(β) is what acts on this next. Unlike Rz, it's not diagonal — it turns |0⟩ and |1⟩ into mixtures of each other:

Rx(β) = c−is −isc
where  c = cos(β/2),   s = sin(β/2)
Read the matrix as two rules: Rx(β)|0⟩ = c|0⟩ − is|1⟩, and Rx(β)|1⟩ = −is|0⟩ + c|1⟩. Every basis state comes out as a mix of both, weighted by c and s.

The mixer applies this to both qubits at once: Rx(β)⊗Rx(β). Next: apply it to each of the 4 terms in |ψ⟩ and see exactly what comes back.

Mixer step 2 of 2

Spreading each term, then collecting by final state

Rx(β)⊗Rx(β) is linear, so apply it to each of the 4 terms in |ψ⟩ on its own — each one fans out into all four final states:

|00⟩  →  c2|00⟩ − ics|01⟩ − ics|10⟩ − s2|11⟩
e−iγ|01⟩  →  e−iγ[ −ics|00⟩ + c2|01⟩ − s2|10⟩ − ics|11⟩ ]
e−iγ|10⟩  →  e−iγ[ −ics|00⟩ − s2|01⟩ + c2|10⟩ − ics|11⟩ ]
|11⟩  →  −s2|00⟩ − ics|01⟩ − ics|10⟩ + c2|11⟩ Each of the 4 starting terms turned into its own 4-way spread — the same Rx split as before, just applied twice, once per qubit. Nothing has been added together yet; this is still 4 separate pieces sitting side by side.

Now add all four spreads together and collect by final state — every final state gets one contribution from each of the 4 starting terms, and those 4 numbers either reinforce each other or cancel. This is the entire point of QAOA in one small, checkable example: the mixer took two states worth 0 cuts and drained them to zero probability, while pushing all of the probability onto the two states worth 1 cut — the actual MaxCut answer for a 1-edge graph. The real 6-qubit circuit runs the identical mechanism — spread each term, add up whatever lands on the same final state — just with 64 terms landing on each target instead of 4. γ and β are exactly what the optimizer searches over: the angles that make high-cost states' contributions add up and low-cost states' contributions cancel, the way this example does by design.

What this means for the code, in full

Two functions, two jobs. problem_hamiltonian_operator — CX, Rz(−γ), CX per edge — deposits a phase that encodes the cost function but changes no probabilities. mixer_hamiltonian_operator — one Rx(β) per qubit — is what reads those phases back out as interference, turning "encoded but invisible" into "measurably more likely." Run both, p times, and that's the whole circuit.
