What this attempt actually showed — and didn't

A from-scratch QAOA implementation for MaxCut, built and tested on a 6-node graph in QAOA_MaxCut.ipynb. This page closes it out: what was verified, what was corrected along the way (with help from Gemini and Claude Web catching real errors), and what the honest bottom line is on quantum advantage.

Cross-checked, not just asserted Every number below was either run directly in the notebook, pulled from a cited source (IBM's own published Heron specs and its 2026 Qiskit Summer School course material), or corrected after independent review from Gemini and Claude Web. Where something is still an estimate rather than a measurement, it's tagged estimated — there are three of those left, listed at the end, deliberately kept short.
1

What this build proved

measuredQAOA's core promise holds

On the exact (noiseless) simulation, expectation value climbed toward the true optimum of 7 as depth increased — exactly the behavior the algorithm promises:

depth 1: 5.2956  →  depth 2: 6.0589  →  depth 3: 6.4362
depth 4: 6.6885  →  depth 5: 6.7214   (true max: 7.0)
measuredA working recipe on simulator, under shot noise

Under realistic shot noise (8192 shots/circuit, still on qiskit-aer — not real hardware), Nelder-Mead and SPSA were both flaky and expensive. COBYLA was cheapest but flaky on its own — fixed by warm-starting each depth from the previous depth's best parameters plus 3 restarts. Result: clean, monotonic convergence, depths 1–10:

5.3157 → 6.0854 → 6.4277 → 6.6879 → 6.8408 → 6.8687 → 6.9004 → 6.9199 → 6.9146 → 6.9150
total circuit executions across all 10 depths: 3,219  (an earlier run landed at 3,195 — restarts 2 and 3 use random jitter, so the total varies run to run by roughly ±1–2%, always around 3,200)
measuredThe most-sampled bitstring never got it wrong

Added a tracker inside expectation_measured that records the single most-frequently-measured bitstring on every circuit call. Checked across all 30 depth/optimizer runs (Nelder-Mead ×5, SPSA ×5, plain COBYLA ×10, warm-started COBYLA ×10):

30 / 30 runs — the top bitstring was always 011001 or 100110
(the graph's only two true-optimal colorings, exact bitwise complements of each other — even at depth 1, expectation as low as 5.29)

Why this holds: the true optimum (cost 7.0) is tied only 2 ways, while the next-best tier (cost 6.0) is tied 5 ways. A single wrong bitstring has to out-sample each of the two leaders individually while splitting its own tier 5 ways — so even a mildly-biased distribution keeps the true optimum in first place, well before the average expectation looks convincing. This is a property of this graph (few ties at the top), not a general QAOA guarantee — a graph with more degenerate optimal solutions would make this far less reliable.

Why there's no advantage claim here

verified against literatureClassical MaxCut is not brute force

An earlier draft of this page compared quantum timing against naive exponential brute force and found a "crossover" around n=30. That comparison was against the wrong opponent. Real classical MaxCut solvers:

methodguaranteetypical time
Goemans-Williamson (SDP)≥87.8% of optimaln = 1,000+ in seconds
Local search / restartsno guarantee, empirically strongn = 1,000+ in ms–seconds
Exact (branch & bound + SDP)certified optimaln ≈ 100–150 in seconds–minutes

Verified directly: a hand-rolled local search with 1 restart got stuck at n=12 (found 13, true optimum was 15) — but with 20 restarts it hit the true optimum on 100% of graphs small enough to check against brute force (n≤20). Cheap, fast, uncertified, and reliable in practice. Against this real opponent, there is no n at which this build's QAOA implementation wins.

The last row comes from IBM's 2026 Qiskit Global Summer School Lab 4b, whose bonus section runs the exact same pattern as this notebook — scipy.optimize.minimize(method="COBYLA") submitting one hardware job per function evaluation inside a Session — and quotes a measured 32 seconds per iteration on real Heron r2 hardware. At Heron's real shot rate, the shots themselves would only take ~7.5s of that 32s; the remaining ~24s is fixed per-iteration job/session overhead that doesn't shrink much with circuit size. Applied to this build's 3,219 iterations: 22–29 hours — roughly 130–175× over the 600-second/month free hardware budget.

None of the three notebooks examined across this whole exercise — this one, the course's own teaching lab, or IBM's official 2026 summer school material — claims to have demonstrated quantum advantage on MaxCut. The course lab's own closing argument for "speedup potential" rests on comparing to exhaustive search, the same weak baseline this page's first draft used and later corrected.

Bottom line This build correctly demonstrates QAOA's core mechanism — more layers genuinely produce better answers, and a properly warm-started, multi-restart COBYLA loop extracts that reliably even under real shot noise, with the most-sampled bitstring landing on the true answer in every single run tested. It does not demonstrate, and was never close to demonstrating, any timing or scale advantage over classical MaxCut solving. Real classical solvers handle instances 1,000× larger than this graph in milliseconds; the honestly-costed quantum side, using IBM's own published hardware numbers, would need most of a day just to train the 6-qubit example here. Every independent check applied to this analysis — two other AI models, direct literature search, and IBM's own course material at far larger scale — landed on the same conclusion. The value of this attempt was in understanding how QAOA works and how to make it reliable, not in beating anything.

What's still genuinely uncertain

  1. estimated The 3,219-execution count was only measured at n=6. Whether it stays roughly constant at larger n (the assumption behind treating quantum time as "flat") has not been tested.
  2. estimated The ~25–32s/iteration hardware-overhead figure comes from a different, larger IBM circuit (30–40 qubits, 3 batched sub-circuits). Applying it to this build's smaller, single-circuit iterations is a reasoned extrapolation, not a direct measurement.
  3. estimated None of this models real hardware noise or connectivity (SWAP overhead, gate-error accumulation). Even the corrected ~22–29 hour timing says nothing about whether the answer that comes back would be trustworthy.
