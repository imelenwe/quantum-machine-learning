MaxCut · what the dots and lines stand for

Same puzzle, three different costumes

The demo showed you the puzzle in its bare form: color the dots, cut as many lines as you can. Nothing about dots and lines is specific to graphs, though — the same exact game shows up wearing very different clothes. Here are three real ones.

The rule, unchanged from the demo Every node picks one of two colors. A line is cut when its two ends land on different colors. The goal is to pick colors so that as many lines as possible end up cut.
Example 1 — Physics

Magnets in a row

Picture four tiny magnets touching each other in a line. Each one points either up or down. In some materials, touching magnets are happiest when they point opposite ways — an up magnet next to a down magnet is calm. Two magnets pointing the same way while touching are fighting each other, straining to flip.

Node
one magnet
Edge
"these two magnets are touching"
Color
which way that magnet points, up or down
Cut
a touching pair pointing opposite ways — calm, stable
Not cut
a touching pair pointing the same way — straining

Finding the calmest arrangement for a whole grid of magnets (not just four in a row) is exactly a MaxCut problem. It's also why the lab notebook writes its cost function using "+1 / −1 spins" — that language comes straight from physics, not from computer science.

Example 2 — People

Seating rivals apart

Four guests are coming to a party, and some pairs of them do not get along at all — sit rivals at the same table and there's drama. You've only got two tables. This is the same spirit as the family-photo puzzle your lab notebook opened with, just with tables standing in for "who ends up near who."

Node
one guest
Edge
"these two guests don't get along"
Color
which table that guest is seated at
Cut
a feuding pair split across two different tables — crisis avoided
Not cut
a feuding pair stuck at the same table — drama

Notice this one managed to cut every single edge — that won't always be possible (the demo's six-guest graph topped out at 7 of 8). Whether a perfect split exists at all depends on the exact shape of who dislikes who.

Example 3 — Engineering

Routers that interfere

Three WiFi routers are placed close enough together that some pairs interfere with each other if they broadcast on the same channel. You've only got two channels to hand out.

Node
one router
Edge
"these two routers are close enough to interfere"
Color
which channel that router is set to, A or B
Cut
an interfering pair on different channels — no interference
Not cut
an interfering pair sharing a channel — still interferes

When every node is connected to every other node (a "triangle" of conflict), two colors can never cut all of it — the best you can do is 2 of 3. This is part of why real WiFi setups lean on three non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) instead of two: a third color would let every pair here separate cleanly.

Same game, different costumes

Magnets want to disagree with their neighbors to stay calm. Guests want to be apart from their rivals. Routers want to avoid sharing a channel with whoever they interfere with. Three different worlds, one identical coloring game underneath — that's the flexibility that makes MaxCut (and QAOA, which searches for the coloring automatically) worth building.

Go build intuition by playing with the interactive puzzle (maxcut_demo.html in this folder) — then come back here whenever you want the plain-English reminder of what problem you're actually solving.
