QAOA proof · the phase gate, derived not asserted

From the exponential to the exact gate

Last time we checked that a specific 3-gate circuit happens to match. This time we go the other way: start from the operator you already built, exponentiate it honestly, and watch this exact gate sequence fall out as the only reasonable answer — six small steps, nothing skipped.

Where we're starting from C(Z) = Σedges (I − ZiZj) / 2     U(C,γ) = e−iγC(Z)
Step 1 of 6

A sum in the exponent becomes a product of gates

e−iγ(A+B) = e−iγA · e−iγB    only when A and B commute (AB = BA) In general, exponentiating a sum of matrices is NOT the same as multiplying the separate exponentials — matrices don't usually commute, so order matters and messy cross-terms appear. There's exactly one clean exception: if A and B commute, the split works perfectly, no correction needed.

Every term in C(Z) is built purely from Z operators, on any qubits. Z operators always commute with each other — even ZiZj and ZiZk, which share qubit i. So this split isn't an approximation for us — it's exact:

e−iγC(Z) = e−iγ Σedges (I−ZiZj)/2 = ∏edges e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2 This is the single most important move in the whole proof: it means we never need one giant gate for the whole graph. We can build the circuit one edge at a time, in any order, and just multiply the small pieces together.
Step 2 of 6

Zoom into one edge

Take one factor from that product and split its exponent into two pieces:

e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2 = e−iγ/2 · e+iγZiZj/2 The first factor is just a plain number — identical for every possible state, since it doesn't depend on i or j at all. A phase that's the same everywhere is called a global phase, and it's invisible to any measurement — physically meaningless. Drop it.
This is the exact same move as dropping the constant when we turned the Ising energy into the MaxCut cost formula, all the way back at the start. It keeps showing up because it's a genuinely reusable trick, not a one-off.

What survives is the real work for this one edge:

e+iγZiZj/2
Step 3 of 6

What that surviving piece actually does

ZiZj is diagonal — you already know its eigenvalue on any state: +1 if i and j agree, −1 if they disagree. Exponentiating a diagonal matrix is easy: exponentiate each diagonal entry on its own, nothing fancier.

agree (00, 11)+1e+iγ/2
disagree (01, 10)−1e−iγ/2
This is the actual target now, stated in plain terms: nudge every "agree" state by +γ/2, and every "disagree" state by −γ/2. The question becomes concrete: is there a real 2-qubit circuit that does exactly this?
Step 4 of 6

Testing the candidate circuit

Here's the candidate: a CNOT, a single-qubit Rz(θ) on the target only, then a second CNOT to undo the first.

Rz(θ) = e−iθ/20 0e+iθ/2
Top-left acts on |0⟩, bottom-right acts on |1⟩ — that's what "leave |0⟩ at e−iθ/2, tag |1⟩ with e+iθ/2" actually means, read straight off the diagonal. The off-diagonal zeros are just as important: they mean Rz never mixes |0⟩ and |1⟩ into each other, it only ever multiplies each by its own phase.

Track all 4 starting states through the 3 gates using that matrix:

So this circuit gives: agree → e−iθ/2, disagree → e+iθ/2. Compare to Step 3's target (agree → e+iγ/2, disagree → e−iγ/2) and solve:

−θ/2 = +γ/2   ⇒   θ = −γ Both rows of the table agree on the same answer — one unknown, two consistent equations. The circuit matches the target exactly when the Rz angle is negative gamma, not gamma itself.

Exact match, both columns. This was also checked directly against Qiskit's own circuit matrix (built the 3-gate circuit, compared it to the hand-derived one numerically) — not just algebra on paper.

Worth knowing: the original lab notebook uses Rz(+γ) instead of Rz(−γ). That's not a mistake — it builds a sign-flipped version of the same idea, and since the classical optimizer searches over every value of γ anyway, it finds the equivalent answer either way. We're using −γ because we derived it ourselves and it's the version that matches our formula exactly, not because +γ would have failed.
Step 6 of 6

Reassembling the full operator

Walk back up the chain: one edge's gadget, with the angle now pinned down, is:

e−iγ(I−ZiZj)/2  ≡  CX(i,j) · Rz(−γ)j · CX(i,j)    (up to the dropped global phase from Step 2)

And Step 1 said the whole operator is just the product of these, one per edge, in any order:

U(C,γ) = e−iγC(Z) = ∏(i,j) ∈ edges   CX(i,j) · Rz(−γ)j · CX(i,j)

What this means for the code

The whole proof collapses into one sentence: for every edge in the graph, apply CX, then Rz(−γ) on the second qubit of that edge, then CX again — the exact same gadget, once per edge, back to back. That's the entire content of the phase_operator function we're about to write.
