VQE, Step by Step

VQE finds the lowest energy of a Hamiltonian. This page explains the idea, works one example by hand, and describes what was built and what it showed.

1 · The picture

One loop between two computers

Dials θnumbers for the gates
→
Quantum chipmakes the state |ψ(θ)⟩, measures the energy ⟨H⟩
→
Optimiserpicks new dials with a lower energy
↺ the loop repeats until the energy stops going down

Purple = quantum work. Teal = classical work.

The quantum chip does only one job: it makes a state and measures its energy. A normal computer does all the "thinking". It decides how to turn the dials. This split is what "variational" and "hybrid" mean.

2 · What it does

Why a lower number is always safe

A Hamiltonian H is the energy rule of a system. Its smallest possible energy is the ground energy, E₀.

The variational principle says: any state has an energy equal to or above E₀.

⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ ≥ E₀ for every state |ψ⟩

Concrete case. Take H = 3X − 2Y + Z. Its ground energy is E₀ = −3.742. The state |0⟩ has energy 1 (worked out in Section 4). Then 1 ≥ −3.742, so the rule holds.

Rule. When the optimiser finds a lower energy, that energy is closer to E₀. The energy can only approach E₀ from above.

Limit. With shot noise, a measured energy can fall below E₀. A real energy cannot do this. So a value below E₀ tells you that noise is present (Section 6).

3 · The four parts

State, energy, optimiser, principle

Part 1: the state (the "ansatz")

An ansatz is a circuit with dials. It starts from |0⟩ and makes a state |ψ(θ)⟩.

For one qubit, the ansatz is two gates: Ry(θ), then Rz(φ).

  • Ry(θ) sets the "latitude" on the Bloch sphere: how much |0⟩ and how much |1⟩.
  • Rz(φ) sets the "longitude": the phase between |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Together, these two dials reach every point on the Bloch sphere. So this ansatz can make every one-qubit state, including the ground state.

Part 2: the energy, measured one Pauli term at a time

H is a sum of Pauli terms: X, Y, Z, each with a number in front. The energy splits in the same way, because an average of a sum is the sum of the averages:

⟨H⟩ = 3⟨X⟩ − 2⟨Y⟩ + 1⟨Z⟩

So the chip never measures H as one thing. It measures ⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩ and ⟨Z⟩ separately. Then the normal computer adds them with their numbers.

The chip can measure only along Z. To measure X or Y, rotate that axis onto Z first, then measure:

To measureRotate first withWhy
Znothingthe chip already measures along Z
XRy(−π/2)moves the X axis onto the Z axis
YRx(+π/2)moves the Y axis onto the Z axis

After the measurement, count the results. Outcome 0 counts +1. Outcome 1 counts −1.

⟨Z⟩ = (N₀ − N₁) / shots

Concrete case. Measure ⟨X⟩ on |+⟩. Ry(−π/2) turns |+⟩ into |0⟩. All 1,000 shots give 0. So ⟨X⟩ = (1000 − 0)/1000 = 1. This is correct, because |+⟩ points along X.

For a term on many qubits, such as Z⊗Z, use the parity of the bitstring. An even number of 1s counts +1. An odd number counts −1.

Part 3: the optimiser

The optimiser is a normal program. It gets an energy for each set of dials. It must choose the next dials. Section 5 describes the two optimisers that were built.

Part 4: the principle

The variational principle (Section 2) makes the search meaningful. The lowest energy found is an upper bound on E₀.

4 · Worked by hand

One qubit, H = 3X − 2Y + Z

Step 1. Energy of |0⟩. For |0⟩: ⟨X⟩ = 0, ⟨Y⟩ = 0, ⟨Z⟩ = 1.

⟨H⟩ = 3·0 − 2·0 + 1·1 = 1

Step 2. Write the energy as a dot product. Two vectors give the energy:

  • The Bloch vector v = (⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩, ⟨Z⟩). For a pure state, its length is 1.
  • The Hamiltonian vector h = (3, −2, 1). Its length is √(9 + 4 + 1) = √14 ≈ 3.742.
⟨H⟩ = h · v = |h|·|v|·cos(angle) = √14 · cos(angle)

Step 3. Find the lowest energy. cos(angle) is smallest, −1, when v points opposite to h.

v points along h: ⟨H⟩ = +√14 ≈ +3.742 ← highest energy v points opposite h: ⟨H⟩ = −√14 ≈ −3.742 ← lowest energy, E₀

Step 4. Check. The opposite direction is v = −h/√14 = (−0.8018, 0.5345, −0.2673).

⟨H⟩ = 3(−0.8018) − 2(0.5345) + (−0.2673) = −2.4054 − 1.0690 − 0.2673 = −3.7417

Takeaway. For one qubit, VQE has one job: turn the two dials until the Bloch vector points opposite to (3, −2, 1). The answer E₀ = −3.742 is known exactly, so every run can be graded.

5 · What was built

Two ways to get the energy, two optimisers

Two ways to get the energy

  • Exact. Calculate ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ directly from the state. There is no noise. This gives the reference value.
  • Measured. Do the rotate-then-count method of Part 2, with 10,000 shots for each term. Each energy then has a small random error, called shot noise (about ±0.01 to ±0.02 here).

Both optimisers use the same energy method, so the comparison is fair.

Optimiser 1: gradient descent (walk downhill)

Gradient descent finds the slope of the energy for each dial, then steps against the slope.

The chip cannot give the slope directly. So the method nudges one dial up by a small amount ε, then down by ε, and compares the two energies:

slope for θ ≈ [ E(θ + ε) − E(θ − ε) ] / (2ε) new θ = θ − (step size) × slope

With two dials, each step needs 4 energies: θ ± ε and φ ± ε.

The weak spot. The slope is a difference of two noisy numbers, divided by a small number.

noise in slope ≈ 0.01 / (2 × 10⁻⁶) = 5,000

The true slope was only about 2. So with ε = 10⁻⁶, the noise is 2,500 times larger than the true slope. Each "step" jumps hundreds of radians, in a random direction.

Optimiser 2: Nelder–Mead (a crawling triangle)

Nelder–Mead uses no slopes. With two dials, it keeps a triangle of 3 points on the energy landscape. In each round, it moves the worst point:

  1. Reflect. Flip the worst point to the other side of the other two.
  2. Expand. If the flipped point is the new best, go further in that direction.
  3. Contract. If the flipped point is still bad, pull it back toward the middle.
  4. Shrink. If nothing helps, pull the whole triangle toward the best point.

The triangle crawls downhill and becomes smaller near the minimum. A small triangle is a clear signal to stop.

One small idea does all four moves. Each move is "go from one point toward another by some factor". A negative factor gives the flip.

6 · What it showed

Results, with and without shot noise

Problem: one qubit, H = 3X − 2Y + Z. The true lowest energy is −3.742.

Nelder–Meadexact−3.7420.000correct
Nelder–Mead10,000 shots−3.829−0.087too low; the dials are correct

Four lessons

  1. Without noise, both optimisers work. Both reach −3.742 exactly.
  2. With noise, gradient descent depends on ε. ε = 0.3 works. ε = 10⁻⁶ fails completely. On a real problem, E₀ is not known, so you cannot tell which ε is safe.
  3. With noise, gradient descent never stops by itself. The energy jitters by about 0.02 forever, so the "stop when the change is small" test never passes.
  4. An energy below E₀ is noise. Both noisy runs report a value below −3.742, which no real state can have. Each reported value is one noisy reading. Nelder–Mead keeps its lowest reading, and that reading is the luckiest one. The dials are correct, so measure the energy again with fresh shots.

Takeaway. On a noisy chip, prefer an optimiser that needs no slopes, such as Nelder–Mead, COBYLA or SPSA. Judge a run by its final dials, then measure the energy again with fresh shots.

7 · Do not mix them up

VQE and QAOA

Both use the same loop (Section 1). They differ in the problem and in the shape of the circuit.

8 · Limits

Where VQE becomes hard

  • The ansatz must reach the ground state. If it cannot, VQE returns a higher energy. Ry and Rz on separate qubits cannot make entangled states. A two-qubit ansatz needs an entangling gate such as CNOT. That two-qubit case was not done yet.
  • Barren plateaus. For deep or very general circuits, the slopes become exponentially small in the number of qubits. Then the optimiser cannot find a direction.
  • Many terms. A molecule's H can have thousands of Pauli terms. Each term needs its own shots.
  • No guarantee. In general, finding a ground energy is hard even for a quantum computer (the problem is QMA-complete). VQE is a practical method, not a proof.
  • No exact answer to grade against. For one qubit, E₀ = −√14 is known. For many qubits, nobody knows E₀.
